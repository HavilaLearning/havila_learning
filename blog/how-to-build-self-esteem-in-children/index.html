How to Build Self-Esteem in Children: 10 Practical Ways for Parents

Self-esteem develops through relationships, experiences, encouragement and the way children learn to understand themselves.

A child may ask: “Am I good enough?” “Can I do this?” “Why can everyone else do it?” Parents cannot prevent every moment of insecurity, but they can create an environment where children learn that mistakes do not make them worthless and difficulties do not mean they are incapable.

10 practical ways to build self-esteem

1. Listen to your child

Put the phone down, look at the child and give them time to finish. Try “Tell me what happened” instead of immediately dismissing a concern.

2. Praise effort, not only results

Notice persistence, patience, improvement and problem-solving. Say “You kept trying” or “You found another way.”

3. Avoid comparisons

Instead of comparing a child with siblings or classmates, focus on their own progress: “What can you do today that was difficult before?”

4. Give opportunities for independence

Let children organise toys, choose between appropriate options, help with routines, complete simple puzzles and try new activities. Independence creates opportunities to discover, “I can do things.”

5. Let children make mistakes

Mistakes are part of learning. Ask “What happened?” “What can we try differently?” and “Would you like to try again?”

6. Give specific encouragement

Instead of only saying “Good job,” explain what you noticed: “You carefully counted all the blocks” or “You remembered the first three letters.”

7. Give children responsibilities

Age-appropriate tasks such as tidying toys, watering plants, organising school materials or helping prepare food can develop competence and confidence.

8. Create learning moments together

Count fruit, read a story, build a tower, draw, look for shapes or observe nature. Learning can be playful and social.

9. Teach children that their value is greater than performance

School results and skills matter, but a child's worth should not depend entirely on grades, popularity or achievements. For Christian families, Scripture can also support conversations about identity and purpose. Psalm 139:14 says, “I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made.”

10. Be present

Know what makes your child happy, what frightens them, what they enjoy and when something changes. Parental presence means connection and consistency, not perfection.

What if my child has very low self-esteem?

Some insecurity is part of normal development, but persistent or severe emotional difficulties deserve attention. Ongoing sadness, intense anxiety, significant withdrawal, major changes in sleep or eating, substantial decline in functioning, hopelessness or thoughts of death require appropriate professional help.

Building self-esteem through love and family connection is valuable, but it is not a substitute for professional care when a child needs it. For Christian families, faith and appropriate professional support can coexist.

A simple confidence-building activity

Ask your child: “What are three things you are proud of?” Then ask: “What is one thing you would like to learn next?” This shifts the conversation from “Am I good enough?” toward “I am growing.”

Final message for parents

Your child does not need a perfect parent. Your child needs a parent willing to listen, encourage, teach, correct, forgive, play, learn, pray and be present.

Self-esteem is not built through one big speech. It is built through hundreds of small experiences: a conversation, a hug, a second attempt, a story, a shared activity, a responsibility, a word of encouragement and a prayer.

Small moments can become strong foundations.
