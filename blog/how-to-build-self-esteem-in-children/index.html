How to Build Self-Esteem in Children: 10 Practical Ways to Strengthen Their Identity in God

True self-esteem begins when children discover who they are in God's eyes, understand their value and learn that they were created in His image and likeness.

Childhood is a fundamental stage in the development of identity. During these years, children begin to discover who they are, how they see themselves and where they belong within their family and the wider world. Their experiences, the words they hear and the way they are treated all influence the image they develop of themselves.

However, not every child grows up with a positive understanding of their own worth. Some feel inferior, rejected, incapable or less important than others. Others experience insecurity, fear of failure, a constant need for approval or sadness they cannot easily explain.

In some circumstances, these experiences may contribute to deeper emotional difficulties, including symptoms of childhood depression.

One of the most important things parents need to understand is that self-esteem is not built only through praise, encouraging words or recognition of a child's abilities. Its deeper foundation begins with discovering who we are in God's eyes.

The Bible teaches that every human being was created in the image and likeness of God. Every person has a special dignity and worth that does not depend on appearance, abilities, social circumstances or the approval of others.

Children therefore need to learn from an early age that they are loved by God, precious in His sight and that their lives have meaning.

But how can parents help their children develop healthy self-esteem grounded in this truth?

10 practical ways to strengthen children's self-esteem

1. Teach your child to discover who they are in God

True self-esteem begins when children understand who they are in God's eyes.

Before learning to evaluate their abilities, appearance or achievements, children need to discover that their existence has value and meaning because they were created by God.

In Genesis 1:26–27, the Bible teaches that human beings were created in God's image and likeness. This means that every person has a special dignity, regardless of appearance, social circumstances, abilities or recognition from others.

In Psalm 17:8, we find a particularly meaningful expression: "Keep me as the apple of your eye; hide me in the shadow of your wings." Deuteronomy 32:10 also describes God's care for His people, protecting them as the apple of His eye.

These passages help children understand that they are precious to God, worthy of His love and deserving of care and respect.

When children discover this truth, they begin to develop a different understanding of themselves. They learn that their worth does not depend on comparisons, praise, school results or physical appearance.

Everyday situation

A child comes home from school and says: "My classmate is smarter than me. I never do anything well."

How parents can respond

Instead of immediately telling the child that they are intelligent, try to understand what happened. Ask how they felt, what made them think that way and whether someone said something that made them feel sad.

Then talk with your child:

"You know, my child, God created you in a special way. He did not make everyone exactly the same. Every person has different characteristics and abilities. You do not have to be like your classmate to be special. God created you in His image, and you have great value."

Practical activity: Discover my worth

Ask your child to draw a picture of themselves and complete these three sentences:

  1. God created me with love because...
  2. One thing I like about myself is...
  3. One thing I would like to learn to do is...

Help your child understand that their worth does not depend on being better than someone else.

2. Demonstrate unconditional love through your actions

Children need to know that they are loved regardless of their grades, behaviour or abilities.

When parental affection seems to depend entirely on good grades, obedience or meeting expectations, children may begin to believe that they must earn love through their performance.

Love should be demonstrated through concrete actions: hugging, listening, talking, playing, participating and making time for children.

Ephesians 6:4 encourages parents to raise their children with discipline and instruction without provoking them to anger.

Children need to understand that their parents can correct their behaviour without withdrawing their love.

Everyday situation

A child receives school results below expectations and begins to say: "My father will be angry with me. He will not love me anymore."

How parents can respond

Before reprimanding the child, try to understand the difficulties they experienced. Show that you are interested in helping them improve, not simply evaluating their results.

"I love you, and that does not change because of a school grade. We need to understand what happened and find a way for you to improve. I will help you learn."

Practical activity: Love does not depend on grades

For one week, set aside a moment each day to tell your child about a quality you appreciate in them, without connecting it to school results or behaviour.

At the end of the week, talk about how they felt receiving these expressions of affection.

3. Be emotionally present in your children's lives

Physical presence does not necessarily mean emotional presence.

A father or mother can spend many hours at home and still fail to establish a meaningful connection with their children.

Work, financial responsibilities, mobile phones and other commitments can take up almost all of an adult's time, leaving little room for family interaction.

Deuteronomy 6:6–7 teaches that God's principles should be passed on to children throughout everyday life: when sitting at home, walking along the road, lying down and getting up.

Spiritual and emotional education should not be limited to Sundays or church services. It should be part of family life.

Everyday situation

A father arrives home, becomes occupied with his phone and gives his child little attention. The child tries to talk but receives only short answers.

How parents can respond

Set aside time to stop other activities and give your child your undivided attention.

Ask about their day, listen to their stories and participate in an activity they enjoy.

"I want you to know that you are important to me. Even when I have many responsibilities, I need to make time to be with you. I enjoy listening to your stories, and I want to know how you feel."

Practical activity: Our 15 minutes

For seven days, set aside 15 minutes each day for an activity chosen by your child.

You could draw, play, read a story or simply talk.

At the end of each moment, ask your child which part they enjoyed most.

4. Avoid comparing your child with other children

Every child is unique and has different characteristics, abilities and developmental rhythms.

Constant comparisons with siblings, classmates or other children can create feelings of inferiority and communicate that a child's worth depends on being better than someone else.

Philippians 2:3 teaches us to act with humility and to value others with respect.

Parents should help children recognise their own progress instead of constantly placing them in competition with others.

Everyday situation

A child sees their sibling receiving praise for school grades and says: "He is always the best. I will never be like him."

How parents can respond

Explain that every child has different abilities and develops at their own pace.

"You and your brother are different, and that is a good thing. You do not have to do everything the way he does. What matters is discovering what you need to improve and continuing to learn."

Practical activity: My progress

Create a small table with two columns: "What I can already do" and "What I want to learn."

Help your child identify three things they can already do and three more they would like to learn.

Review the table after a few weeks to recognise the progress they have made.

5. Be careful with the words you use

Parents' words can contribute to building or damaging a child's confidence.

A child who frequently hears that they are incapable, useless or never do anything correctly may begin to believe those statements.

Proverbs 18:21 teaches that death and life are in the power of the tongue.

Parents should therefore be aware of how they speak to their children, especially during moments of conflict.

Everyday situation

A child drops a glass and the father, feeling irritated, says: "You always break everything! You never do anything right!" The child becomes upset and begins to believe they are clumsy and incapable.

How parents can respond

Acknowledge that the child made a mistake, but avoid turning that mistake into a definition of who they are.

"The glass broke, and we need to be more careful next time. Mistakes happen, but we can learn from them. Let's clean this up together."

Practical activity: Words that build

Prepare two sets of cards.

On the first set, write negative expressions such as "I cannot do it", "I am incapable" and "I never do anything right".

On the second set, write constructive alternatives such as "I can learn", "I will try again" and "My mistakes help me improve".

Read the cards with your child and discuss the difference between these two ways of thinking.

6. Teach and speak God's Word with your children

God's Word should be part of children's spiritual education and the development of their identity.

It is not enough to teach children Bible verses simply to memorise them. Parents should help their children understand the meaning of Scripture and relate it to the situations they experience.

  • When your child feels afraid, teach them 2 Timothy 1:7.
  • When they question their worth, read Psalm 139:14 together.
  • When they feel alone, talk about Hebrews 13:5.
  • When they face difficulties, teach them Philippians 4:13.
  • When they need to understand God's love, read 1 John 3:1.

Everyday situation

A child asks: "Does God love me? Because sometimes I do things wrong."

How parents can respond

Explain that God loves us, but that this does not mean every action we take is right.

"God loves you, even when you make mistakes. When you do something wrong, you can admit your mistake, ask for forgiveness and learn to do better. God's love does not mean that we do not need to learn and obey."

Practical activity: Learn and declare God's Word

Choose one Bible verse each week. Read it with your child, explain its meaning and ask them to give an example of how they can apply it in their lives.

Encourage them to repeat the verse and express what they have learned in their own words.

For example: "God created me wonderfully. I am important, and I should respect myself and others."

The goal is for children to understand God's Word and learn to live according to its teachings.

7. Correct your children without destroying their confidence

Parenting requires boundaries, discipline and guidance. Loving a child does not mean allowing every behaviour.

However, there is a difference between correcting an action and attacking a child's identity.

Saying "What you did was wrong" is different from saying "You are a bad child."

Proverbs 3:11–12 presents correction as part of instruction and love.

Everyday situation

A child disobeys a parent's instruction and, when corrected, begins to cry and says: "I am always bad. I never do anything right."

How parents can respond

Explain that the behaviour was inappropriate, but that this does not mean the child is bad or unworthy of love.

"What you did was not right, and we need to talk about it. But I want you to know that you are loved. You can recognise your mistakes, ask for forgiveness and learn to make better choices."

Practical activity: Learning from my mistakes

After a conflict, talk with your child about three questions:

  1. What happened?
  2. What could I have done differently?
  3. What can I do to make things right?

Help your child understand that recognising mistakes is an opportunity to grow.

8. Help your child discover and develop their talents

God created every person with unique characteristics, abilities and potential to develop different skills.

Some children show an interest in music, others in drawing, reading, numbers, nature or practical activities.

Parents should provide opportunities for children to discover their interests and develop their abilities.

Everyday situation

A child enjoys drawing but says: "I am not as good as the other children. My drawing is ugly."

How parents can respond

Avoid judging the drawing only by its quality. Recognise the child's creativity, effort and willingness to experiment.

"I like the way you used your imagination. You still have many things you can learn. What matters is that you keep practising and discovering what you can create."

Practical activity: Discover what I enjoy

During one week, introduce your child to different activities:

  • Drawing or painting.
  • Building objects with blocks.
  • Singing or following a song.
  • Planting and caring for a plant.
  • Telling stories.
  • Solving simple mathematical challenges.

At the end, talk about which activities they enjoyed most and encourage them to keep developing those interests.

9. Teach your child to express feelings and seek help

Not every child knows how to explain what they are feeling.

Some hide their sadness, while others withdraw, become irritable or lose interest in activities they previously enjoyed.

Parents should create an environment where children feel safe expressing emotions without fear of being ridiculed or punished for what they feel.

James 5:16 highlights the importance of sharing and praying for one another.

Everyday situation

A child comes home from school, goes straight to their bedroom and does not want to play or talk. When the parents ask what happened, the child replies: "Nothing. Leave me alone."

How parents can respond

Do not immediately force a conversation or dismiss the behaviour.

"I can see that you are not feeling very well today. You do not have to talk right now, but I want you to know that I am here to listen. When you are ready, we can talk."

If the situation continues, try to understand what is happening at school, at home or in the child's relationships with other children.

Practical activity: How do I feel today?

Prepare cards showing different emotions: happiness, sadness, fear, anger, embarrassment and calmness.

Ask your child to choose the card that best represents how they feel that day.

Talk about why they chose that emotion and what might help them feel better.

When signs of emotional distress persist, seek an assessment from a qualified professional. If a child talks about wanting to die or hurt themselves, seek urgent help.

Faith, prayer and family support can be part of helping a child, but they do not replace psychological or medical care when needed.

10. Establish a family routine of communion with God

Spiritual and emotional identity is not formed in a single day. It develops through repeated experiences of love, security, guidance and learning.

Parents should therefore establish a family routine that supports both the spiritual and emotional development of their children.

Deuteronomy 6:6–7 reminds us of the importance of teaching God's instructions throughout everyday life.

Everyday situation

Parents notice that family members spend much of their time doing separate activities. Children use electronic devices, adults are busy, and there are very few opportunities for conversation, prayer or shared learning.

How parents can respond

Establish a daily moment for the family to come together, even if it is brief.

Explain to your children that this is an opportunity to learn, talk, give thanks and strengthen family relationships.

Practical activity: Our moments with God

Set aside 10 to 15 minutes each day for:

  1. Read: choose a Bible passage appropriate for your child's age.
  2. Teach: explain the meaning of the passage through simple examples.
  3. Talk: ask what your child understood and how they can apply the teaching.
  4. Speak: repeat biblical truths together about God's love, identity and the value of every person.
  5. Pray: give thanks, share concerns and ask for God's guidance.

For example, when teaching Psalm 139:14, parents can help children understand that they were wonderfully made by God and do not need to be like anyone else to have value.

When studying Psalm 17:8, they can talk about God's care and protection.

Most importantly, these moments should not become merely a religious obligation, but an opportunity to strengthen the relationship between parents and children and develop a shared spiritual life.

When might low self-esteem indicate a deeper emotional difficulty?

It is important to understand that not every experience of sadness means depression. However, when a child experiences persistent feelings of inferiority, withdrawal, loss of interest, prolonged sadness or significant changes in behaviour, parents should pay attention.

Childhood depression can appear differently from depression in adults. For this reason, persistent changes in a child's behaviour and everyday functioning deserve professional assessment.

Parental presence, teaching God's Word, prayer and family love are important, but they should be accompanied by specialised support when needed.

We should not confuse emotional suffering with a lack of faith or make children feel guilty about what they are experiencing.

Helping also means recognising when professional support is needed.

Conclusion: Help your children discover who they are in God

Building a child's self-esteem is about much more than teaching them to believe in their abilities. It is about helping them discover their identity, recognise their worth and understand that they were created in the image and likeness of God.

Children need to grow up knowing that they are not inferior, that they do not need to imitate others to be accepted and that their worth does not depend on their achievements or the approval they receive.

Parents have a fundamental role in this discovery.

Through daily presence, love demonstrated through actions, balanced discipline and the teaching of God's Word, parents can help their children develop a secure identity and a deeper relationship with their Creator.

Do not wait until your children face emotional difficulties before investing in their spiritual development.

Talk to them. Listen to them. Teach them God's Word. Pray with them and show them every day that they matter.

Remember: your children need to know not only that they are loved by their parents. They also need to discover that they are God's creation, that they reflect His image and that they are precious in His sight, like the apple of His eye.

True self-esteem begins when children discover who they are in God's eyes and learn to live according to that identity.
